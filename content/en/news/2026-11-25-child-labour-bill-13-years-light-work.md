Nepal's government is defending a proposal that would let children aged 13 to 15 do "light work" under certain conditions. It argues that an outright ban on child labour, on its own, will not solve the problem.

A House of Representatives committee — the Industry, Commerce, Labour and Consumer Interests Committee — held a general discussion on the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Bill on Wednesday at Singha Durbar. Minister for Youth, Labour and Employment **Ramji Yadav** answered lawmakers' questions.

## 1.1 million children still working

Yadav said about **1.1 million** children in Nepal are still engaged in child labour. The bill, he said, aims to bring that number down while taking the country's economic, social and family realities into account. He added that the government does not intend to pull children out of school and put them to work.

## What the bill proposes

- **Ages 13–15:** only light work, such as ordinary help to parents at home — and only if it does not affect the child's education, health, or physical and mental development.
- **Ages 15–18:** the types of work allowed, and the limits for each age, would be set out in a schedule to the bill.
- Long hours, work that harms health, or work beyond the age-based limits would be treated as exploitative labour.
- The bill also covers rescue, rehabilitation and reintegration, alongside prohibition.

## Lawmakers want more than a law

Committee members said legal provisions alone will not end child labour. They called for safe shelter after rescue, financial support for poor families, getting children back into school, and regular monitoring.

Lawmaker Gangadevi Shrestha said child labour is increasingly hidden and urged that the entertainment sector, such as dohori venues, fairs and festivals, be covered. Prabha Karki raised domestic child labour and said age disputes should be settled in the child's best interest. Kripa Ram Rana said the bill must be consistent with Article 39 of Nepal's constitution and International Labour Organization Conventions 138 and 182.

## What happens next

Committee chair **Rahabar Ansari** called the bill progressive, saying its fines, legal liability and rehabilitation provisions will hold employers accountable for hiring children. The bill now moves to clause-by-clause discussion, where lawmakers' suggestions are to be incorporated.

The bill has not been passed, and its provisions could still change.

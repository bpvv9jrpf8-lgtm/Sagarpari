The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) has finalized a rule requiring everyone who applies for or lives in HUD-assisted housing to verify U.S. citizenship or eligible immigration status. The rule, titled "Verification of Eligible Status," was published in the Federal Register on November 12.

## What the rule says

According to HUD's announcement:

- HUD will require verification of U.S. citizenship or eligible immigration status for **all applicants and tenants** in covered HUD programs.
- Anyone who declares U.S. citizenship must **provide documentation**.
- Anyone who declares eligible immigration status must **submit evidence** for verification.
- Tenants in "mixed status" households are covered by the rule.

HUD said an audit found nearly 200,000 tenants with incomplete or unknown eligibility verification. "Promises made, promises kept," HUD Secretary Scott Turner said in the announcement.

## When does it take effect?

HUD's announcement does not give an effective date. CBS News reports that the rule is scheduled to take effect on **December 14**.

## What it means for mixed-status families

A mixed-status family is one in which some members are U.S. citizens or eligible immigrants and others are not. CBS News reports that since 1995, such families have been allowed to live together in HUD-funded housing, with assistance covering only the eligible members. Under the new rule, CBS says, some families may have to choose between leaving assisted housing together or splitting up.

Citing an estimate from the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities (CBPP), CBS says about 20,000 mixed-status families — roughly 80,000 people — could be affected, including about 37,000 children, most of them U.S. citizens.

HUD first proposed the rule in February 2026. According to CBS, it drew more than 11,000 public comments, and a coalition of 22 state attorneys general opposed it. Marie Claire Tran-Leung of the National Housing Law Project told CBS the rule "is tearing apart families."

## What Nepali families should do

If you live in public housing or other HUD-assisted housing, read any notice from your local public housing agency (PHA) or landlord carefully. Keep proof of citizenship (such as a U.S. passport or birth certificate) or your green card and other immigration documents ready. If anyone in your household has an unclear status, talk to an attorney or accredited representative before signing anything.

A Nepal Supreme Court order allowing accused people who live abroad to **give their statements to the court by video conference** still hasn't been put into practice six months later. Nepal's embassy in Canberra, Australia, has told the government it has neither the authority nor the capacity to help record such testimony or to enforce any custody order that might follow, The Kathmandu Post reported Friday.

The ruling had been seen as a precedent for cases involving Nepalis overseas, so its fate matters to Nepalis living in the US and other countries as well.

## What the Supreme Court decided

On Aug. 1, 2025, Nepal's anti-graft body, the Commission for the Investigation of Abuse of Authority (CIAA), filed a corruption case at the Special Court. One of the accused is a woman living in Australia on a valid visa. Her lawyers asked that she be allowed to give her statement and appear by video. The Special Court rejected the request by a majority, and the matter went to the Supreme Court.

On May 19, 2026, a division bench of Justices **Hari Prasad Phuyal** and **Balkrishna Dhakal** ruled that accused people living outside Nepal may give statements and appear in court by video conference. According to Kantipur, the bench said the process could be completed through Nepal's embassy in the relevant country, and that the electronic record must be stored only on the court's official encrypted server.

## Why the embassy said no

On Aug. 7, the Special Court asked the Ministry of Foreign Affairs to have the embassy in Australia arrange the recording. Embassy officials first phoned the accused to ask whether she would come in. But in a Sept. 16 letter, acting ambassador **Tara Gautam** gave four reasons the embassy could not do it, according to the Post:

- The embassy has no judicial jurisdiction, courtroom or detention facility.
- With limited staff, it cannot provide round-the-clock police guard.
- A person legally living in Australia on a valid visa cannot be forcibly detained solely under Nepali law.
- Australia takes privacy and human rights seriously.

Most Nepali missions abroad run on small staffs, and some do not have their own buildings.

## What happens next

Special Court information officer **Parbati Hitan** said the case will move forward regardless. The embassy and ministry letters will go before the bench, and the next hearing is set for **Dec. 7**. The accused's lawyer, Keshav Bhattarai, argues that being unable to enforce a custody order should not be a reason to refuse to record her statement. The foreign ministry says it is coordinating on implementation and has exchanged letters to clear up open questions.

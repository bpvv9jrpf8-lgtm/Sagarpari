The University of Michigan has asked the federal government to withdraw a proposal that would stop immigration officers from relying on earlier approvals when they review visa extensions. In a notice released on Wednesday, the university said it had filed a formal comment opposing the plan. The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) published the proposal on October 14, and the public comment period ends Friday, November 13.

## What would change

Under the current policy, when the employer, the worker and the facts are the same, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) officers generally defer to their own earlier approval when deciding an extension request. If the proposal is adopted, that requirement would go away. Officers could review an extension from scratch without explaining why they reached a different conclusion than before.

The university says this would add both uncertainty and burden for people seeking extensions. It says it supports careful review of petitions, but argues the change is unnecessary because USCIS already has the power to correct cases that were approved by mistake.

## Who is affected

The proposal covers all extension petitions filed on Form I-129, which includes temporary work visas such as H-1B, L-1, O-1, E-3 and TN. By the department's own estimate, an average of 208,429 petitions a year from fiscal years 2022 through 2025 were eligible for deference to a prior approval — about 30 percent of all I-129 petitions. The department also acknowledges that ending the policy would lead to more Requests for Evidence (RFEs), raising paperwork and costs for both employers and workers.

For Nepalis and other foreign professionals in the U.S. on H-1B and other work visas, this matters directly: even with the same employer and the same job, every extension could be examined as strictly as a first-time petition.

## What has not changed

This is still only a proposal. The current rules stay in place until a final rule is published and takes effect, and no one's existing approval is canceled by it. The proposal also does not touch the protection that lets workers who file an extension on time keep working for the same employer for up to 240 days while it is pending.

After the comment period closes, the department will review the feedback and decide whether to issue a final rule, revise the proposal or take another step. That makes this the last formal chance for workers, employers and immigration lawyers to weigh in.

Many workers in the US will soon be signed up for a retirement plan without filling out a single form. The Treasury Department and the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) published final rules in the Federal Register on Wednesday, November 18, spelling out how newer 401(k) and 403(b) plans must automatically enroll employees.

The requirement comes from the SECURE 2.0 Act, passed in 2022. The final rules give employers and plan administrators detailed instructions on how to apply it.

## What the rules require

- **Automatic payroll deductions:** In covered plans, eligible employees start saving automatically. The starting rate must be the same for everyone and must be **at least 3% and no more than 10%** of pay.
- **Yearly 1-point increases:** Unless the employee chooses otherwise, the rate rises by at least 1 percentage point each year until it reaches at least 10%. Automatic increases cannot go past **15%**.
- **Right to opt out or change:** Employees can choose not to contribute at all or pick a different percentage.
- **Getting money back:** Workers who don't want to save after automatic deductions begin can elect a "permissible withdrawal." Under the law, that election must be made within 90 days of the first automatic contribution.

## Who is exempt

Not every plan is covered. Plans set up before December 29, 2022, businesses that have existed for less than 3 years, small businesses that normally employ 10 or fewer workers, government and church plans, and SIMPLE 401(k) plans are exempt.

## When it takes effect

The regulations take effect January 19, 2027, and generally apply to plan years beginning after December 31, 2027. The law's auto-enrollment requirement itself already applies to plan years beginning after December 31, 2024; for those earlier years, the rules say a reasonable, good-faith interpretation standard applies.

## What it means for you

If you start a new job or your employer launches a new 401(k), you may see 3% or more of your paycheck go into retirement savings automatically. That builds savings for the future but trims your take-home pay. If a 401(k) deduction shows up on your pay stub, ask HR about the rate, any company match, and how to opt out. How much to save — or whether to save at all — is your decision.

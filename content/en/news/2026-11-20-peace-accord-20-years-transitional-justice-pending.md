Saturday, November 21, marks 20 years since Nepal signed the Comprehensive Peace Agreement (CPA) that ended the Maoist armed conflict. Yet victims of wartime killings, enforced disappearances, torture and sexual violence are still waiting for justice. Both of Nepal's transitional justice bodies — the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) and the Commission of Investigation on Enforced Disappeared Persons (CIEDP) — currently have no commissioners.

## ICJ: victims carry the cost of the state's failure

Ahead of the anniversary, the International Commission of Jurists (ICJ) urged the government to set up both commissions without delay, [The Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2026/11/20/nepal-s-conflict-victims-still-await-justice-20-years-after-peace-deal) reported. Mandira Sharma, the ICJ's Asia and Pacific regional director, said that two decades after the agreement, conflict victims "continue to bear the burden of the State's failure to deliver justice."

The ICJ is asking the government to:

- Form the TRC and CIEDP through merit-based appointments
- Bring the law in line with Nepal's international obligations
- Ensure investigations and prosecutions
- Guarantee that victims can take part in the process
- Provide reparations to victims

## A UN expert has made the same call

On November 7, Bernard Duhaime, the UN Special Rapporteur on truth, justice, reparation and guarantees of non-recurrence, also called on Nepal to quickly appoint commissioners so both bodies can resume work. According to [The Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2026/11/07/un-expert-urges-nepal-to-revive-stalled-transitional-justice-process), his planned visit to Nepal this year could not go ahead because of the delay in appointments, and he said he would seek new dates.

## Where the process stalled

The conflict lasted from 1996 to 2006. Parliament passed a law creating the two commissions in 2014. In February 2015, the Supreme Court struck down several of its provisions and ordered amendments, and Parliament amended the law in 2024.

In May this year, the new government decided to cancel the process of appointing commissioners, leaving both bodies empty. According to the ICJ, the commissions received tens of thousands of complaints, but neither has produced meaningful results.

Many Nepali families now living abroad were also affected by the conflict. When the commissions get new members and resume investigating complaints now depends on the government.

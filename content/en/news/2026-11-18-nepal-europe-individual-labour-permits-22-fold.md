The number of Nepali workers getting individual labor permits to work in Europe has grown about 22-fold in recent years, a new study finds. But the same route has exposed workers to more fraud, heavy debt, irregular migration and exploitation.

The study, "Navigating the European Corridor: Emerging Trends and Dynamics of Nepali Labour Migration," was commissioned by Germany's GIZ and carried out by the Centre for the Study of Labour and Mobility (CESLAM) at Social Science Baha, The Kathmandu Post reported on Nov. 17.

## Why the individual route grew

In March 2023, Nepal's Foreign Ministry told its missions in Europe to stop attesting foreign employers' demand letters. Since then, the study says, more job seekers have turned to individual permits and informal middlemen instead of licensed recruitment agencies.

## Key numbers

- Europe's share of all Nepali labor permits rose from 3% in fiscal year 2020/21 to about 8% in 2024/25, according to Department of Foreign Employment data.
- CESLAM research director Jeevan Baniya says the share is now above 10%.
- More than 83,000 Nepali workers went to Europe between 1990 and 2024, including 32,268 in 2024 alone.
- Newspaper job ads list service fees of Rs 10,000 to Rs 80,000, but an analysis of media reports found workers using individual channels actually paid Rs 200,000 to Rs 1.5 million.

## Where workers are going

New destinations include Romania, Croatia, Poland, Malta and Albania, along with Cyprus and Portugal. Romania and Cyprus account for a large share of formal job demand. Some workers later move on to Portugal or Spain.

## Fraud risks

Agents use Facebook, WhatsApp and TikTok to lure workers with forged visas, fake work permits and fake contracts, the study says. The Department of Foreign Employment has received a growing number of complaints against agents and has issued some arrest warrants.

## Recommendations

The study calls for pre-departure training for Europe, more migration resource centers, resuming demand-letter attestation after verification, a larger consular presence, bilateral labor agreements, closer monitoring of agencies, consultancies and online platforms, and action against hundi (informal money transfer) networks.

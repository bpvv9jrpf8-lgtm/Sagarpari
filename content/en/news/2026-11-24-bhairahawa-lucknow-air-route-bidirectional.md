Nepal has won its first concrete breakthrough in a 17-year push for more air entry routes through India. During Prime Minister Balendra Shah's state visit to India, the two countries agreed on Monday to make the Kathmandu–Bhairahawa–Lucknow route, known as B345, operate in both directions. Today it runs one way only.

According to The Kathmandu Post, the joint press release issued after the visit says: "Both sides agreed that the existing unidirectional B345 Kathmandu-Bhairahawa-Lucknow route will be made bidirectional." It adds that technical consultations will be held to finalize the details and to take up other civil aviation proposals.

## Why it matters

International flights coming to Nepal from the west currently have to enter mostly through the Simara entry point. The Post reports that flights from the west to Bhairahawa detour about 300 km via Simara. Tourism Ministry documents show westbound international flights to Pokhara fly roughly 185 km extra. That has made flights into Gautam Buddha International Airport in Bhairahawa and Pokhara International Airport costlier and less attractive for airlines.

Nepal has been asking for additional entry routes since 2009. During Prime Minister Narendra Modi's 2014 visit to Nepal, officials were directed to settle the issue within six months, but it stalled.

## What's still pending

The biggest open question is altitude. To land at Bhairahawa, aircraft need to descend while still in Indian airspace, and India has not yet said how low they may fly. For the Simara entry, the altitude at the border is set at 21,000 feet.

Buddha Air chairman Birendra Bahadur Basnet called the agreement commendable but said "we should not celebrate until we know the technical details." Former government secretary Suresh Acharya, who led Nepal's air route talks in 2014, called it "a breakthrough" and said Bhairahawa's feasibility will depend on the altitude India permits.

Next, technical teams from both countries will carry out an assessment, after which the two civil aviation authorities will sign an Air Traffic Services (ATS) Letter of Agreement. Officials say approval from ICAO is not needed because the route is already international. One official told the Post the route could be running within six months if the process is fast-tracked.

The altitude on a separate route, Kathmandu–Mahendranagar (L626), is still disputed. India has mainly proposed 39,000 feet, while Nepali technical officials want lower altitudes allowed as well.

For Nepalis abroad, the deal could eventually mean more direct international flight options into Bhairahawa and Pokhara — but how soon depends on how quickly, and on what terms, the technical details are settled.
